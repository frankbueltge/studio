## A — The alert that could not be recalled: Hawaii, 13 January 2018

At **8:07 a.m. HST** the Hawaii Emergency Management Agency transmitted, over the Wireless Emergency
Alert and Emergency Alert System, to every phone and broadcast receiver in the state:
**"BALLISTIC MISSILE THREAT INBOUND TO HAWAII. SEEK IMMEDIATE SHELTER. THIS IS NOT A DRILL."**
The correcting Civil Emergency Message went out at **8:45 a.m. — 38 minutes later**. An unofficial
correction ("NO missile threat to Hawaii") was posted on the agency's Twitter account at 8:20, thirteen
minutes in, reaching whoever happened to be looking at that account. The FCC's report: *"this false
emergency alert resulted in 38 minutes of confusion, fear and uncertainty for the residents of Hawaii"*
and *"neither the false alert nor the 38-minute delay to correct the false alert would have occurred had
Hawaii implemented reasonable safeguards and protocols."*

- FCC, *Report and Recommendations: Hawaii Emergency Management Agency January 13, 2018 False Alert*,
  April 2018 — <https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-350119A1.txt> **[verified first-hand
  2026-08-04]**
- FCC preliminary report — <https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-348923A1.pdf> **[UNVERIFIED]**

**The structure:** one instruction, delivered to a whole population by an apparatus nobody opted into,
carrying the exact words *this is not a drill*; the correction delivered 38 minutes later by the same
apparatus, to a population that had already acted. What people did in those 38 minutes cannot be undone
by the 8:45 message.

## B — The claim that outran its own retraction

A paper published in a general medical journal on 22 May 2020 was retracted within weeks — the
retraction notice carries the date 13 June 2020 in the index (<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32450107/>,
verified first-hand 2026-08-04). In the six
calendar years since, it has been cited at **1,145 sites published after the retraction date**, and at
**777 of them the retraction notice does not appear in the citing work's reference list at all** —
**67.9%**. The full count, its method, its proxy, its limits and the five URLs a stranger re-runs:
**`COUNT-64.md` in this directory** (run first-hand tonight, before any form was chosen).

The stricter published measure of the same phenomenon: Hsiao T-K & Schneider J, *Continued use of
retracted papers: Temporal trends in citations and (lack of) awareness of retractions shown in citation
contexts in biomedicine*, **Quantitative Science Studies** 3(4), 2022, doi:10.1162/qss_a_00155 — of
**13,252 post-retraction citation contexts**, **722 (5.4%)** acknowledged the retraction.
<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9520488/> **[verified first-hand 2026-08-04]**

**The structure:** the correction exists, is public, is machine-readable, is attached to the object by
the index itself — and two thirds of everyone who picked the claim up afterwards never carried it.

## C — Sixty-one thousand charges, dismissed years late

Two Massachusetts state drug-lab chemists falsified evidence over roughly a decade. The convictions
built on their certificates were dismissed **en masse, years after the sentences were served**: the ACLU
of Massachusetts, its co-counsel and the state's public-defender agency report **more than 24,000
convictions dismissed in 16,449 cases** in the second of the two actions, and **more than 61,000
dismissed charges across more than 37,000 cases** for the two combined.

- ACLU of Massachusetts, press release, 25 September 2019 —
  <https://www.aclum.org/press-releases/report-shows-more-24k-wrongful-convictions-dismissed-drug-lab-scandal/>
  **[verified first-hand 2026-08-04]**
- The Commonwealth's own page on the two labs states that the Dookhan dismissal covered **"over 20,000
  cases in Eastern Massachusetts"**, effective **20 April 2017**, and that the Amherst dismissal was
  expanded to **"all cases tested at the Amherst Drug Lab between January 2009 and January 2013"**,
  effective **13 December 2018** — <https://www.mass.gov/info-details/drug-lab-cases-information>
  **[verified first-hand 2026-08-04]**. The more precise figure circulating in press accounts (21,587
  convictions) is **[UNVERIFIED]** and may not be used in a dossier before its own source is opened;
  the state's *"over 20,000"* is the figure available tonight.

**The structure:** the first instruction was a signed certificate stating what a substance was. It
travelled instantly and completely into a person's life — a plea, a sentence, a record. The correction
arrived as a docket entry after the fact and restored none of the time.

## D — Records that were cleared and did not go away

A court order sealing or expunging a criminal record clears the state's file; it does not reach the
private databases that copied it. The Markup's *Locked Out* series reported tenant-screening reports
carrying sealed and expunged records, and one leading screening company paying **more than $1 million**
to settle a federal class action in 2018 over reporting expunged information. *"Data has a tendency to
travel."* Pennsylvania's "lifecycle file" — weekly court updates pushed to the data brokers under
contract, with audits — is named there as the mechanism that mostly prevents it.

- Lauren Kirchner, *When Zombie Data Costs You a Home*, The Markup, 6 October 2020 —
  <https://themarkup.org/locked-out/2020/10/06/zombie-criminal-records-housing-background-checks>
  **[verified first-hand 2026-08-04]**
- National Consumer Law Center research on criminal-records screening accuracy **[UNVERIFIED]**

**The structure:** the correction is real, legally binding, and issued by the body with authority over
the original — and it does not reach the copies, because nobody's system was built to carry a deletion.

## E — The recall that arrives after the device is inside the body

Recalled implanted or in-home medical devices whose users are not reached: recall notices routed by post
between manufacturers and hospitals, arriving weeks or months late or at the wrong desk; patients
learning of a 2021 recall of a breathing machine months or years afterwards and continuing to use it.
The US Government Accountability Office reported in 2026 on limitations in the oversight of the recall
process.

- GAO-26-107619, *Medical Device Recalls: HHS and FDA Should Address Limitations in Oversight of Recall
  Process*, issued **8 December 2025**, publicly released 12 December 2025 —
  <https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107619/index.html> **[verified first-hand 2026-08-04]**. Its
  findings include that **74 per cent of recalls exceeded the agency's three-month termination goal**,
  that *"insufficient staff limit FDA's ability to conduct oversight activities"*, and that recall
  letters have been sent to the wrong person; it also records that manufacturers and the agency
  sometimes failed to communicate updated risk information directly to consumers when device failure
  rates increased **after** the recall.
- MedTech Dive, *"Where's the patient?": Experts question FDA's final recall guidance* —
  <https://www.medtechdive.com/news/experts-question-fdas-final-recall-guidance/621530/> **[UNVERIFIED]**

**The structure:** the correction has a physical addressee who cannot be reached by a document, and the
prior condition is not restorable in principle — the object is inside a body.
**Note for the ranking: this house has already worked a recall register** (`projects/cpsc-recall-channel/`,
killed session 62). Any proposal advancing E must say what is different, or it is the same shelf.

## F — Corrections that lose the race in public

The measured asymmetry between a false claim's spread and its correction's: Vosoughi S, Roy D & Aral S,
*The spread of true and false news online*, **Science** 359, 2018 —
<https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aap9559> **[UNVERIFIED]**. Widely reported findings include
false stories being retweeted substantially more than true ones and reaching audiences an order of
magnitude larger.

**The structure:** the most general and best-known version of the shared problem — and therefore the one
most exposed to *"competent, honest, already known"*, which the takedown law says does not open a
project.
