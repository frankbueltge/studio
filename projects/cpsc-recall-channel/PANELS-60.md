*Run by the conductor. Fifteen readers in total: ten on the room's pre-registered agency test, five on
the score. **Readers are not voices.** They hold no opinion about this work, vote on nothing, and are
counted separately from the role sub-agents, per the convention session 57 established. None of them

## PANEL 1 — THE ROOM'S AGENCY ORGAN (the staging voice's pre-registered test of session 59 §11)

**Pre-registration, quoted from `STAGING-RULING-59.md` §11, written before any stimulus existed:**

> **Pre-registered here:** severed readers, one residue stimulus in two versions — **(A)** wreckage
> alone; **(B)** wreckage with each piece beside its destruction photograph and its refund sum —
> question *"What happened here, and who did it?"* Coded **OWNER** where the answer names the object's
> owner or a private person as the actor; **OTHER-ACTOR** where it names a company, an inspector, the
> government, an accident or vandalism; **NO-ACTOR** otherwise. **My claim: (B) returns OWNER ≥ 4 of 5
> and (A) ≤ 1 of 5.** If (B) does not beat (A) by three readers, **the receipt is not the organ, my
> §2.1 repair is wrong, and the room's agency defect is unsolved by anyone — in which case the room is
> dropped and only the score survives.** Let the record show I said it before anyone ran it.

The Artist adopted that consequence as its own before the run (`ARTIST-SCORE-60.md` §(c)): *"If (B)
does not beat (A) by three readers, the receipt is not the organ, and I drop the room and keep only the
score."*

### The conductor's one edit to the stimuli, declared

The Artist's stimuli both opened *"There are no signs, labels, screens or printed text anywhere in the
room"* — which stimulus B then contradicts by placing printed photographs and printed sums on the
floor. The phrase was replaced, **identically in both**, with *"There are no wall texts, signs or
screens anywhere in the room."* A/B parity is preserved and the only difference between the two
stimuli remains the receipts. Nothing else was altered.

### Stimulus A — residue alone

> A room about six metres by eight, concrete floor, white walls, no windows, evenly lit from above.
> There are no wall texts, signs or screens anywhere in the room.
>
> On the floor, spread out in no pattern and not touching, are about twenty household items, each
> damaged. A foam mattress cut in half. A nursing pillow in two pieces. A metal bed rail with its
> straps cut through. A wooden step stool with two legs sawn off. A plastic drain cover with a word
> written across it in black marker. A small metal cylinder submerged in a bowl of water. A child's
> high chair with the tray snapped off. A dozen smaller plastic and fabric objects, each cut, split or
> broken in a similar way.
>
> Near the centre of the room one item stands undamaged and complete, resting on the floor at the
> height it would be used at.
>
> A visitor can walk between the items. Nothing is behind glass and nothing is fixed down.

**Stimulus B** is identical, with one paragraph inserted before the last two:

> Beside each damaged item lies a printed photograph, about postcard size, showing that same item in
> that same damaged state, taken indoors. Under each photograph is one printed line giving a date and
> an amount of money.

**Question put to all ten, unchanged:** *"What happened here, and who did it?"*

### The ten answers, verbatim

**A1.** "An artist has deliberately destroyed a collection of domestic care items—the tools of
parenthood and childcare—leaving them scattered across the floor like casualties of some quiet
catastrophe. The single undamaged object at the center suggests what remains functional or what's being
preserved in the face of this systematic destruction. This reads as a stark, visceral installation about
the damage done to motherhood, caregiving, or childhood itself—whether through trauma, exhaustion,
systemic failure, or loss. The deliberate nature of the destruction and the emptiness of the room
amplify the absence of what these broken things represent."

**A2.** "Someone has systematically destroyed everyday care items—mattresses, safety rails, high
chairs, nursing pillows—that are used for children and vulnerable people, and arranged them across the
floor as a deliberate statement. The single intact object in the center creates a stark contrast: it
might represent the ideal state these things should be in, or it might be the tool that broke everything
else. This reads as a protest or memorial about the breakdown or violence within care systems—either
institutional neglect, personal abuse, or the deliberate dismantling of safety infrastructure. The
accessibility of the pieces (nothing protected or elevated) suggests the artist wants you to witness the
specific damage up close and draw your own conclusion about what happened."

**A3.** "An artist systematically destroyed everyday objects designed for care and support—mattresses,
railings, high chairs, pillows—rendering them useless and arranging them as a visual statement. The
intact object at the center stands as either a survivor or a silent question posed back to the viewer.
What happened is a deliberate demolition of care infrastructure and dependency, presented without
explanation to provoke reflection on vulnerability, loss, and the fragility of the systems that support
us."

**A4.** "An artist destroyed the infrastructure of care. This is an installation of dismantled support
systems—objects designed to enable independence for children, elderly people, and those with mobility
needs—all methodically cut, sawn apart, and rendered useless. The undamaged item at the center creates
an ironic focal point, emphasizing what's been lost. By leaving these objects unprotected and
walkable-through, the artist forces viewers to literally navigate among the remains of care systems,
making visible and tangible what happens when support is systematically withdrawn."

**A5.** "An artist deliberately damaged and arranged household items associated with childhood care and
safety—mattresses, pillows, bed rails, high chairs—scattering them across a stark white room to create
an installation about the violation or loss of protection within domestic spaces. The single undamaged
item standing at its use height at the center acts as a focal point that amplifies the meaning of the
destruction surrounding it, inviting visitors to walk through and contemplate what these damaged objects
represent: the fragility or breakdown of the systems meant to safeguard children."

**B1.** "An artist has created an installation documenting household damage, collecting broken items
(notably care equipment like nursing pillows, high chairs, and bed rails) and displaying each alongside
a photograph of itself damaged, with a date and monetary value stamped below. The systematic
arrangement—items scattered and touchable, nothing protected—transforms the everyday economics of loss
and insurance claims into an artwork, while the one undamaged item standing alone at the center serves
as a kind of memorial to what was intact before. This reads as an examination of fragility, care, and
how we assign financial value to the destruction of the objects that sustain vulnerable daily life."

**B2.** "Someone experienced substantial damage to their household items—likely from a break-in, fire,
accident, or other disaster—and meticulously documented each damaged piece with photographs and
insurance claim information (dates and compensation amounts). They've now presented this documentation
as an artwork, transforming the bureaucratic, emotional process of itemizing loss into a gallery
installation that makes visible what insurance reduction typically hides: the systematic conversion of
broken things and disrupted life into dates and money. The single undamaged item at the center marks
what survived, or stands as a stark contrast to the surrounding destruction."

**B3.** "This is a parent's documentary record of accumulated damage caused by raising a young child—the
nursing pillow, high chair, bed rail, and step stool all suggest a household with an infant or toddler.
Each photo, date, and replacement cost is evidence of relentless, everyday destruction: the child broke
or damaged each item, the parent documented it (photographically, financially), and moved on. The single
undamaged item positioned in the center, posed as if about to be used, represents either grim
resignation—this one will break too—or the child themselves, the source of all this inevitable damage.
The artist is showing us what parenting actually looks like stripped of sentiment: not precious moments,
but a trail of broken things."

**B4.** "An artist has created a conceptual installation documenting the inevitable damage and
replacement cycle of household items, particularly those used in childcare. The damaged objects
scattered on the floor—the broken mattress, high chair, nursing pillow—each paired with a photograph and
dated record of replacement cost, form an archive of domestic breakage over time. The single undamaged
item at the center stands as a kind of marker or starting point, emphasizing the contrast between
functional and broken, and making visible the material attrition that normally remains invisible and
unrecorded in daily life. This is a work about loss, cost, and the physical traces of living."

**B5.** "A caregiver has documented the wear and devastation of caregiving—the household items
(mattress, high chair, bed rail, nursing pillow) are the tools of caring for a dependent person, each
one damaged through use or breakdown, with the photographs and date-plus-amount records tracking the
cumulative cost and toll of this labor over time. The single undamaged item at the center likely
represents the person who needed this care, or what remains when caregiving ends. It's a visual
accounting of an intimate, exhausting process usually invisible or taken for granted."

### The coding — and the gap in the pre-registered code, declared before the verdict

The pre-registered code has no cell for the answer that ten readers actually gave. **"An artist"** is a
private person, but it is not *the object's owner*, and it is not on the OTHER-ACTOR list (a company, an
inspector, the government, an accident, vandalism). The code is therefore **underspecified for the
modal answer**, exactly as KC2(a) was on session 59's stimulus. This house does not choose the flattering
reading. Both readings are printed and the verdict is taken under each.

| reading | what counts as OWNER | panel A | panel B | B − A |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **LITERAL** (the code's words: the object's **owner**) | the person whose possession it was, named as the actor | **0 of 5** | **0 of 5** | **0** |
| **PRIVATE-PERSON** (any named private individual as the actor of the damage) | an artist, a parent, a child, an owner | **5 of 5** | **1 of 5** (B3, the child) | **−4** |

**Under both readings the pre-registered claim fails, and under the second it fails inverted.** The
claim was (B) ≥ 4 of 5 and (A) ≤ 1 of 5, with a required margin of three readers. B does not beat A by
three under any reading available; under the private-person reading **A beats B by four.**

**The finding, which is not merely a failed threshold.** The receipts did not make the destruction read
as an act. They made it read as an **accident with compensation**: insurance claims (B1, B2), a
break-in, fire or disaster (B2), a child breaking things (B3), replacement cost and material attrition
(B4), wear and breakdown through use (B5). **The money line was read as money received for damage
suffered, never as money received for damage performed.** Adding the sum moved five readers from
*someone did this deliberately* to *something happened to these and someone was paid.*

**And what neither panel produced, in ten readings out of ten:** no government, no recall, no notice, no
instruction, no obedience. Not one reader named an authority of any kind as the source of the act.

**Verdict, in the pre-registered vocabulary: the receipt is NOT the agency organ. The room's agency
defect is unsolved by the staging voice's repair, by the Artist's improvement on it, and by anyone
else in this house.** The consequence was written by both of them before the run and is adopted below.

## PANEL 2 — IS THE SCORE PERFORMABLE BY SOMEONE WHO IS NOT US?

**What it tests.** The staging voice's condition on whether this is a work at all: *"A score is a work
if someone who is not us can perform it holding nothing but the score."* Five fresh severed readers,
handed the score's ten clauses and nothing else, asked: **(1)** in one sentence, what is this?
**(2)** if you decided to do what it says, what exactly would you do first, today, in your own home?
**(3)** is there anything in it you could not do without help from whoever wrote it?

**A construction error of the conductor's, stated before the results.** The stimulus was the ten clauses
alone. On the sheet as the Artist wrote it, the source URLs stand **below the rule**, outside the score
and outside the read-aloud. The panel therefore tested **the score as heard**, not the sheet as handed
over. That is my error in building the stimulus, it is not what I set out to test, and it changes what
question 3's answers are evidence for. They are printed anyway, because what they show is real and was
not obtainable another way.

### The five answers, in summary and with the load-bearing lines verbatim

**Q1 — what is this.** 5 of 5 read the mechanism correctly: a procedure or performance about product
recalls, in which you check a household object, follow the recall's own remedy, document it and keep the
record. 3 of 5 named it art unprompted ("a conceptual art instruction set" · "a performance piece" · "an
art performance or conceptual work"). **No reader mistook it for a government document** — the failure
mode that killed the screen version, where 5 of 5 read our page as the state's own.

**Q2 — the first action, today.** **5 of 5 named a concrete object they would pick within the hour**:
"a coffee maker, toothbrush, or kitchen knife" · "a coffee maker or toothbrush" · "a coffee maker or
lamp" · "a toaster, a hair dryer, a lamp, or a kitchen appliance" · "a coffee maker, a kitchen knife, a
charging cable, or a light switch." **Binding condition 1's first-clause test passes, 5 of 5, on a
reader who had never seen us.**

**Q3 — could you do it without the author.** **0 of 5 said they would need us.** S2: *"No. The sheet
directs you to use the Consumer Product Safety Commission's public recall database and follow
instructions that would be printed in the recall notice itself—both publicly available resources. You
wouldn't need to ask the author for anything."* S4: *"Mostly no—you could find the Commission's (CPSC)
public recall database independently… The instructions are designed to be followable using publicly
available information."* **Two of five resolved "the Commission" to the right agency with nothing but
the clause.**

**But 4 of 5 named the same gap**, and it is worth more than the pass: as heard, the score does not say
*which* Commission or where the record is. S1: *"I'd need access to whichever 'Commission's public
recall record' this refers to. That exists outside the sheet and I couldn't locate it alone."* S3: *"The
sheet refers to 'the Commission's public recall record' without saying which commission or where to
access it."* S5: *"You'd need to know how to access 'the Commission's public recall record' (probably
the CPSC database, but the sheet doesn't explain where or how)."*

**What this is and is not evidence for.** It is **not** evidence that the sheet is unperformable — the
URL is on the sheet, below the rule, and none of these readers were shown it. It **is** evidence that
the score **read aloud** is not self-sufficient, which matters for a work whose own condition is that it
be readable aloud in under sixty seconds. A listener who hears all ten clauses cannot begin. The repair
is one clause-length and costs nothing; it is named as owed rather than made tonight, because the
Artist's files are frozen and this house does not let the conductor rewrite a work's face after its
panel has run.
